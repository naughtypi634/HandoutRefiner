## 1 Non-financial rewards are better motivators than financial ones.

That old chestnut: **how important is money?**

Before starting, you could ask the students to make a list of the
non-financial rewards that motivate them, such as holidays, flexibility,
a fulfilling job, and a positive work environment, and then let students
compare answers.

Afterwards, you could try to rank the rewards in order of importance as
a class.

## 2 It's not okay to exaggerate on your CV.

How much should you sell yourself on your CV?

On the one hand, the job market is competitive and we should give
ourselves the best shot at landing a job.

On the other hand, honesty and integrity are important and exaggerating
could lead to problems in the future.

An extremely fun topic if students are willing to share their personal
experiences.

## 3 The best employees don't always agree with the superior's decisions and often challenge them.

This poses an interesting question: **Does the superior always know
best?**

A team whose members are free to voice their disagreements Vs. a team
whose members are quiet and do as they're told.

This debate becomes very insightful when students bring up their
personal experiences, especially those with a history in management or
working in teams.

## 4 A four-day workweek is the future

Work less, but smarter.

This concept is growing in popularity at the moment... but will it
happen?

Of course, it would provide all kinds of benefits to the individual
worker, but what about the consequences for the businesses and the
economy?

## 5 Remote workers are more productive

The remote work debate has been heating up ever since the pandemic of
2020.

**Remote workers** save time by not having to commute to the office and
have more flexibility with their schedules. But is it more difficult to
maintain a healthy work-life balance?

Whereas **office workers** may find it easier to maintain a clear
separation between their work and personal lives. But are they affected
by wasting time on their commute and lack of freedom?

There are plenty of arguments for both sides on this one.

## 6 Most meetings are a waste of time

A great way to open this up is to ask students two questions:

- How many hours do you spend in meetings each week?

- Do you think this time could be spent doing more productive things?

This immediately starts the conversation on the pros and cons of
organising meetings.

## 7 Companies should block social media sites on work computers

A good opportunity to discuss different companies' policies on this.

Should employees be free to choose whether they spend work time on
social media? Or is it best to remove this decision and take a more
authoritative approach?

## 8 Multitasking is the only way to get things done

This a hot issue because we're all guilty of multitasking to some
degree.

One side can argue that there aren't enough hours in the day to balance
our work, family, and social commitments.

However, multitasking prevents us from performing at our best and can
lead to high levels of stress and anxiety.

## 9 Casual dress codes are better than formal dress codes

This makes a great topic to debate because it often splits opinions
right down the middle.

How important is it for companies' employees to look professional?

Could casual dress codes help staff be happier, more comfortable and
more productive?

## 10 Employees should still come to work even if they are sick

The disruptions caused by sick absences Vs. the employee\'s well-being
under pressure to work.

For most, this is an easy disagreement***.*** But don't let that get in
the way of a good debate. Let students have fun by taking on the role of
a strict manager who expects his employees to come to work even if they
have the flu.
